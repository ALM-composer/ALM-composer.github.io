Anders Lauge Meldgaard · works · about · listen

Guitarden of Eden · 2026

In the autumn of 2026, I composed a new work for the guitar sextet CRAS, entitled Guitarden of Eden. During the compositional process, I became interested in incorporating a simple choreography that would allow the guitarists to move around the performance space. The entire work, lasting approximately 20 minutes, is performed standing. At times, the musicians remain in fixed positions; at others, they move slowly through rotating patterns around the room. The choreography is predetermined and closely connected to the musical material. I wanted to move away from the conventional image of six guitarists sitting statically behind their music stands, and instead create a freer framework for the performance. There is something inherently vulnerable about asking musicians to move while playing: they lose the support of their music stands, and both performers and audience become more exposed. Yet I found that this vulnerability brought a particular sense of intimacy and presence to the concert, opening up a more poetic space around the music. I experienced the work unfolding in a new and exciting way for the audience. This experience challenged a belief I had held for most of my life: that my music should be performed in complete darkness, because music is, above all, something to be listened to. I consider myself a highly auditory person, but I have come to realise that for many people, perhaps even most, the visual experience is an essential part of attending a concert. During my studies at the conservatoire, a fellow student once told me that “most people listen with their eyes”. At the time, I didn't give the remark much thought. Now, however, I have experienced first-hand the significance of the visual dimension in the encounter with live music. The experience of Guitarden of Eden has inspired me to explore what happens when movement becomes not merely a performance practice, but an integral part of the compositional process itself. This opens up three central questions in my ongoing artistic exploration: Movement as a compositional parameter. How does the musical language change when musicians move rather than remain seated behind music stands? How does movement affect texture, tempo, dynamics and spatial placement? And what happens to the sound when the distance between instrument and listener becomes fluid and constantly changing? Complexity and playability. How can I write music that can be performed while musicians are moving? What happens to musical complexity when conventional notation is replaced, or supplemented, by memory, signals or physical cues? And how can movement be notated as an integral part of a composition rather than an additional layer? The boundary between support and distraction. When does movement enhance the listening experience, and when does it draw attention away from the sound itself? I am interested in how movement can become an entrance into the auditory experience rather than a competitor to it. By experimenting with different degrees of movement, from almost imperceptible gestures to more pronounced spatial shifts, I want to investigate the point at which movement opens up the music — and the point at which it begins to interfere with it.
